## What this means for Metabolic Reset

**It explains why our current ads read as generic.** The playbook's "doesn't stop the scroll" list (p.26) is a fair description of most of our deck: a product on a plain background, a logo, anything that looks like an ad. Our copy also misses its three tests (p.15): there's no pattern interrupt, no curiosity gap and no specific benefit. "Considered on the inside" fails all three.

**Worth taking:**
- **Specificity (p.21, p.35).** We now have real specifics, and none of them are in the ads yet: six actives including berberine and resveratrol, two capsules a day with breakfast, pharmacist formulated, an amber jar, 60 capsules. Specific lines like "the two capsules we take with breakfast, and what's in them" beat lines like "biohacking, beautifully".
- **The Secret Info / Reveal format (p.31) and the Native Highlight (p.30).** A close crop of our own box panel, with a hand-drawn circle around one line, suits a brand whose angle is "read the label". It's native, specific and on-brand.
- **The Raw Native format (p.29).** iPhone-feel shots of the jar in a real kitchen or bag. Our location shoot (0683, 0694, 0604, 0898) is close to this already.
- **The slippery lead-in (p.32).** The first 1 to 3 lines should continue the headline's curiosity and end mid-thought. Ours restate the headline.
- **Long copy as targeting fuel (p.35, p.38).** Andromeda reads the copy to decide who sees the ad. Our short lowercase lines give it almost nothing. Write longer primary text at about a grade 3 to 5 reading level, up to 2,200 characters, naming the person ("women 30 to 45 who train and eat well") and the moment ("the 3pm slump after a good lunch") without making a health claim.
- **"Learn More" CTA (p.37).** The playbook says it beats Shop Now and Sign Up. Test it against our planned "Shop now" at launch.
- **Account structure (p.47 to p.60).**
  - **Campaigns:** run one Launch campaign and one Scale campaign per product, with Advantage+ broad targeting and the ads mixed together, not split into ad sets by angle. That challenges our C1 to C5 and L1 to L4 split: nine campaigns on a waitlist budget fragments learning.
  - **Targeting and tracking:** exclude purchasers through both the Pixel and a Klaviyo sync, and use 7-day click with 1-day engage attribution.
  - **Bidding:** cost cap at target CAC × 1.15, and a budget set at twice the average daily spend once the cost cap is proven.
- **Retargeting (p.53, p.61 to p.64).** For a simple, low-price product, the playbook says Meta's broad targeting retargets better than separate campaigns do. Keep one waitlist-to-buyer campaign (L3) and fold the site-visitor retargeting (L4) into broad, unless we go subscription-first.
- **The operating habits.**
  - Three split tests running at all times.
  - The landing page headline should match the ad headline.
  - Generate variants of each winner rather than riding it until it dies, and run a zombie campaign for ads Meta never spent on.
  - Diagnose an ad before deleting it.
  - Track net cash (blended MER) over 30 days.

**Not worth taking as written, because of NZ/AU supplement rules and the brand:**
- **Weight-loss and outcome headlines.** Lines like "melt 20 to 50kg" and "lose 12 to 19kg in 5 weeks", breaking-news "bombshells", shock faces and before/after splits. Therapeutic advertising rules don't allow weight-loss or outcome claims, timeframes or before/afters, and they would undo the "fashion object" position.
- **Testimonial-led proof ads.** Ads like the lozenge one ("one dose, coughing is gone") rely on health-outcome testimonials. Use proof that's about the product and the routine (label, ritual, design, reviews about taste and habit), not about results.
- **Tabloid styling.** Red arrows on everything, Daily Mail headline structures. Borrow the mechanics (specific, native-looking, curiosity, long lead-in), not the tabloid tone. If we test tabloid-style formats at all, keep them in one small, labelled test cell.

## Page 8 — Section 02: Meet Andromeda (footer 07)
- Diagram (Source: Meta Engineering, December 2024): User Requests → Ads Corpus → Hierarchical Ad Index + Hierarchical Model → Ad Candidates, running on model/system co-design on Meta MTIA and the NVIDIA Grace Hopper platform (Grace CPU, Hopper GPU).
- Andromeda is the part of Meta that picks which ads might fit you. It runs first, before anything else.
- How it works:
  - Someone opens Facebook or Instagram. That is a "user request."
  - Meta has tens of millions of ads in a giant pile: the "ads corpus."
  - Andromeda sorts those ads into groups ("like folders inside folders"): the "hierarchical ad index." Smart AI models do the sorting quickly.
  - Out comes a short list of ads that might fit you: the "ad candidates." A second system then picks the winners from that short list.
- It runs on purpose-built chips (Meta MTIA, NVIDIA Grace Hopper) in a fraction of a second, every time someone opens the app.

## Page 10 — Section 03: The Larger Market Formula (footer 09)
- Buyer pyramid for any market: **3% Buying Now**; **17% Information Gathering Mode**; **20% Problem Aware**; **60% Not Problem Aware**.
- Most advertisers fight over the 3% with "buy now" offers, retargeting bombs and discount codes. That market is brutal: bidding is expensive and margins get crushed.
- Why they love Meta: you can target the entire pyramid. Put the message in front of people whether or not they're thinking about the problem. The job is to articulate problems they're experiencing or might experience, then run them through a funnel that warms them up, nurtures and educates them until they're "ready, willing, and able to buy."
- "You're not just hunting the 3%. You're farming the 97%." / "That's the entire game."

- Every ad has just two jobs:
- **Job 1: Deliver news, value, or findings**, because that's what people want to consume.

## Page 15 — The Three Components (footer 14)
- Every hyperdopamine ad has: **1. Pattern Interrupt** (an image weird enough to stop the scroll) + **2. Burning Intrigue** (in both the image and the headline) + **3. Specific Big Benefit** (aimed at your exact market) = **Hyperdopamine Ad**.
- Checklist for any ad:
  - Does it interrupt the pattern? Is there something weird, unusual, native or impossible to ignore?
  - Does it create burning intrigue? Is there a curiosity gap that only a click can close?
  - Does it promise a big specific benefit? Will the target market self-identify and say "I want that"?
- Scoring: "Three for three = hyperdopamine. Two for three = mediocre. One for three = dead on arrival."

## Page 21 — The Swipe-and-Adapt Method + One Note on Specificity (footer 20)
- "Every headline you see in the wild can be adapted to your offer." Examples:
  - Original: "Taylor Swift fans go wild after spotting X-rated detail on singer during Eras Tour concert" → Adapted: "Aussies go wild after spotting X-rated detail in this home loan hack"
  - Original: "Insane new documentary leaves viewers screaming, knocks Baby Reindeer off the top spot" → Adapted: "Insane new mindset trick leaves anxiety sufferers speechless"
  - Original: "Secret way to find out who called you without using Google" → Adapted: "Secret way to find $5M of business funding without bank"
- "You're not stealing. You're tapping into a structure proven by billions of impressions."
- **One Note on Specificity**: "Generalised claims = doubtful readers."
  - Doubted: "Here's how to lose weight" vs Believed: "Here's how to lose 12–19kg in the next 5 weeks"
  - Ignored: "Explode your leads" vs Clicked: "Generate 90–480 inbound leads every month"
- "The more specific, the more believable. The more believable, the higher the CTR."

- "The single most overlooked part of every Meta ad."

## Page 34 [footer 33] — Body copy rules 2–6
- **Rule 2: Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs.** One- and two-syllable words; **8–12 word sentences**; **1–2 line paragraphs**. People will give flak for line-by-line copy that breaks English-class rules — ignore them; "the data is overwhelming." Eye relief = consumption; consumption precedes conversion.
- **Rule 3: Write to one person, not a group.** Not "our customers", **you**. Not "King Kong does X", **we do X**. A one-to-one letter, not a brochure.
- **Rule 4: Avoid "you" and "your" excessively.** Platforms dislike calling out individuals directly (triggers classifiers around personal attributes). Use sparingly; replace with "people who…" or "anyone who…" where possible.
- **Rule 5: Lead with self-interest.** Why does the prospect care / keep reading? If they don't see their self-interest served, "they're gone in 0.4 seconds."
- **Rule 6: Positive hooks beat negative hooks 8 out of 10 times.** Underperforms: "Don't put on 50kg this winter, do this one thing". Outperforms: "Melt 20–50kg with this one weird hack". Even in pain-driven markets the big-benefit positive frame typically wins — test both, default to positive.

## Page 35 [footer 34] — Body copy rules 7–9
- **Rule 7: Add zest, personality and specificity.** Generalised claims breed doubt. Bake in real numbers, names, timeframes. Show personality — readers are sick of corporate-speak.
- **Rule 8: Theme everything around one angle.** Once you find a winning headline, align image, lead-in, body and CTA around that one angle. Don't say five things in one ad — "Pick the bullseye and send it."
- **Rule 9: Long copy is targeting fuel.** The algorithm reads the copy. Andromeda reads image, copy, offer and implicit signals, then matches against data on every user. More context = wider, more accurate buyer pool.
  - Short copy = small context window = small audience pool.
  - Long copy = big context window = big audience pool.
  - Stack in the niche ("dental practice owners doing $1M–$5M") and the specific symptom ("Beer Belly Didn't Budge… Until I Tried THIS"). Every specific = algorithm fuel; every "maximise your business potential" vagueness = targeting heat lost.
  - "This is why 'short and punchy' loses to 'long and interesting' on Meta" — not only persuasion, but short copy starves the algorithm of targeting data.

## Page 38 [footer 37] — Section 13: The One-Keyword Hack: Creative is the Targeting
- "Here's the shift that broke targeting wide open." With Andromeda the creative IS the targeting: Meta reads image, copy, offer and landing page and matches the signal against user data. Whoever is implied in the creative is who Meta hunts for.
- So instead of stacking interests in the ad set, put the targeting IN the creative.
- **How it works:** take your best winning ad; look at the 20% of customers driving 80% of revenue — which niches, which avatars dominate? Duplicate the winning ad and swap **one word** in the headline or lead-in: the niche name, demographic or avatar.
- Generic: "Here's how to get 462 leads per week on autopilot." → Niche-injected: "…462 dental leads per week on autopilot", "…462 property investment leads…", "…462 lawyer leads…", "…462 chiropractor leads…".
- Same offer, same skeleton, one word changed — Andromeda reads "dental" and hunts people whose data says "dentist", opening a pocket of buyers a generic ad couldn't reach.
- "It's not a magic word. It's a category word, an identity trigger. And most advertisers leave it out entirely."

## Page 40 [footer 39] — Section 14: The AI Variant Engine: From 1 Winner to 100 Ads
- Common mistake: find a winner, kill everything else in the ad set, ride that one ad until it fatigues and dies, then panic and start from scratch. "1-star. Don't do this. Would not recommend."

  3. Prompt variants by audience: "Rewrite for a 25-year-old woman." / "…45-year-old woman." / "…55-year-old man." Bones stay the same; avatar-specific signal changes.
  4. Start with body copy, then apply the same logic to headlines, then to image briefs.

## Page 53 (footer 52): Section 19: Exclusions: The Klaviyo/CRM Trap
- Every adset must exclude existing purchasers, for two reasons:
  1. It stops "fluff" sales (people who would have bought anyway from email or organic) from being counted as ad-driven.
  2. It forces Meta to keep prospecting net-new customers.
- **The trap:** most advertisers exclude only via the Pixel audience. The Pixel audience misses about 40% of existing customers: anyone who bought before the pixel fired correctly, anyone who used a different email or device, anyone tracked imperfectly.
- **You must exclude via two audiences (callout):**
  1. Pixel Audience: purchasers tracked via the Meta pixel.
  2. Klaviyo Sync Audience (or other CRM): the full customer list synced from Klaviyo/CRM to Meta.
- "Set up both. Exclude both." Otherwise you show ads to your own customers and count their next subscription renewal as "ad-driven revenue". Especially critical for subscription brands and high-LTV accounts.
- **On retargeting, default to off, except where it earns its keep:**
  - Do not exclude website visitors. Do not exclude social engagers.
  - For low-AOV, simple e-commerce, do not run a separate retargeting campaign. Meta's broad algorithm now retargets better than you can manually.
  - For high-LTV businesses, high-AOV funnels and lead gen (multi-touch journeys), strategic retargeting with different offers, objection-handling ads and proof can lift performance. Framework is in Section 26.

## Page 54 (footer 53): Section 20: Attribution Settings That Tell the Truth
- Most accounts default to 7-day click + 1-day view attribution.
- "That 1-day view setting is a lie." Most 1-day view conversions are not incremental: they're people who would have bought anyway and happened to scroll past the ad in the last 24 hours.
- **King Kong runs 7-day click + 1-day engage attribution on every campaign.**
  - 7-day click captures real ad-driven sales.
  - 1-day engage captures people who genuinely interacted (paused, watched, clicked the post). No 1-day view fluff.
- **Heads up:** switching from 1-day view to 1-day engage makes reported numbers look worse on the surface. They're not worse, just more truthful, and actual business performance doesn't change. For a like-for-like comparison with old campaigns, use Columns → Compare Attribution Settings.
- Visual: a screenshot of the Ads Manager attribution setting.
  - "After clicking ad": 7-day click selected over 1-day click. Note: "Use 7-day click to capture real ad-driven sales."
  - "After clicking or viewing ad": 1-day engage selected over 1-day click or view. Note: "Use 1-day engage to capture genuine interactions (no 1-day view)."

## Page 55 (footer 54): Section 21: How to Group Ads Inside an Adset
- **Old bad practice:** group by angle or format (Adset 1 = breaking news ads, Adset 2 = SMS ads, Adset 3 = raw native ads).
- **New best practice:** don't group at all. "Mix and mash."
- If you have 15 new ads ready, put all 15 in one adset. Don't overthink it. The algorithm now performs better with diverse adsets than segmented ones.
- **How many ads per adset:** default 10–20.
  - Account struggling with low ROAS → consolidate to 20–40 ads per adset. Concentrating signal helps the algorithm find winners.
  - Account struggling to find new winners → segment to 5–10 ads per adset. More adsets = more independent learning.
- **Flexible format:** launch small variations of one creative (e.g. 3 hook variations, 4 image colour variants) as one ad using Flexible format, not separate ads. The algorithm tests variations without splitting your signal.

## Page 56 (footer 55): Section 22: Bid Strategy: Cost Cap vs ROAS Goal
- "This is the part most people screw up."
- Default Meta campaigns use Highest Volume bidding: Meta spends the whole daily budget regardless of performance. On a bad day it spends the budget anyway; on a profitable day it hits the ceiling and stops.
- **"We don't run Highest Volume. We run Cost Controls."** Cost Controls (Cost Caps and ROAS Goals) tell Meta "Only spend money if you can hit our target." That means:
  - Meta spends less on bad days (minimises unprofitable spend).
  - Meta spends more on good days (maximises profitable spend).
  - Meta only spends on ads that work (kills wasted testing spend).
- **Which bid strategy where:**
  - Launch campaign → Cost per Result Goal (Cost Cap). If Cost Cap consistently overspends and Sculpting Bids (next section) don't fix it, fall back to **Bid Cap**.
  - Scale campaign → ROAS Goal. ROAS Goal is value-optimised: it captures sales that volume-optimised Cost Cap would miss, usually higher-AOV customers. Running both at once across Launch and Scale gives broader audience reach than either alone.
  - If ROAS Goal consistently overspends and sculpting doesn't fix it → fall back to Cost Cap.

## Page 57 (footer 56): How to Set Your Bids
- Two concepts first:
  - **Target CAC** = target Customer Acquisition Cost.
  - **Target ROAS** = target Return on Ad Spend.
  - **Incrementality Factor** = the gap between what Meta says it caused and what it actually caused.
- For most brands, Meta drives roughly 15% more revenue than it reports (on 7-day click + 1-day engage attribution). Default to a 1.15 Incrementality Factor unless a geo-lift test says otherwise.
- **Now plug in (formulas):**
  - Cost per Result Goal (Cost Cap) = Target CAC × 1.15
  - ROAS Goal = (Target ROAS ÷ 1.15) × 1.10
  - Bid Cap = Target CAC × 1.15 × 1.15
- Adjust the Incrementality Factor over time based on blended new-customer ROAS (aMER).

## Page 58 (footer 57): Section 23: Sculpting Bids
- Ideally a 3.0 ROAS goal delivers a 3.0 ROAS. In reality Cost Controls drift, overshoot or undershoot, especially when changing accounts, during creative refreshes, or in seasonal volatility.
- **Diagnose before you sculpt:** Cost Controls aim for a 14-day average, not daily. Day-to-day swings are normal because Meta attributes purchases to the day of purchase, not the day of ad view.
  - Example: Monday, Meta spends a lot at low ROAS. Wednesday, a chunk of Monday's viewers buy. Wednesday's reported ROAS looks great, but the spend was Monday's.
  - Look at at least 7 days of data before deciding to sculpt. The exception is an extreme outlier (3.0 ROAS goal, 1.0 actual, spending heavily): then act fast.
- **How to sculpt:** if the ROAS or cost target consistently misses after 7+ days, adjust the goal by 10–20% to correct.
  - Example: true target ROAS 3.0; ROAS goal in Meta 3.0; actual ROAS 2.1 (way under). Action: raise the ROAS goal to 3.6. Result: actual ROAS pulls up to 3.0.
  - "You're not lying to the algorithm. You're calibrating it."
  - If sculpting still doesn't fix it after another week, abandon the inflated-budget approach and manage the old-school way: raise or lower budgets manually and kill underperforming adsets.

## Page 59 (footer 58): Section 24: The Inflated Budget Strategy
- Feels backwards, so most people get it wrong. In a Cost Control campaign the daily budget is a ceiling, not a target. Meta only spends up to the budget if it can hold your CAC/ROAS target.
  - Example: a $5,000 daily budget where the algorithm can only profitably spend $3,000. It spends $3,000 and the other $2,000 stays in the bank.
- **Rule: always set the budget higher than you think you'll spend. Default: Budget = 2× Average Daily Spend.**
  - Spending $10k/day on average → set the budget to $20k.
  - Spending $2.5k/day → set it to $5k.
- This gives Meta room to spend more on great days (audience hot, CPM low, conversions flowing). Without the inflated ceiling, you cap your own upside on your best days.
- **Heads up:** when launching a brand-new campaign, start with a low daily budget and confirm the Cost Control is actually working. Once it's validated as hitting target, move to the inflated budget.

## Page 60 (footer 59): Section 25: Running Multiple Countries Without Blowing It Up
- Rule: if gross margin, repurchase rate or inventory situation varies significantly between countries → **segment by country**.
- Example structure: USA Launch, USA Scale, AU Launch, AU Scale.
- If unit economics are roughly the same across countries → keep them grouped together at adset level.
- Don't create separate campaigns just because you can. Each country split fragments learning, so only split when the underlying economics demand it.
- Visual: a world map with the USA and Australia highlighted green and labelled "USA, Launch / USA, Scale" and "AU, Launch / AU, Scale".

## Page 61 (footer 60): Section 26: Strategic Retargeting: Different Offers, Not Different Pressure
- Reminder from Section 18: low-AOV, simple e-com doesn't need a separate retargeting campaign because Adv+ Audience handles it.
- For high-LTV businesses, high-AOV funnels, lead gen and any multi-touchpoint journey, strategic retargeting squeezes much more revenue from the same top-of-funnel spend. Done right, it turns every $1 of ad spend into $3–$5 of revenue.
- **The wrong way (what most people do):** take the same ad they already saw, retarget non-converters with it, slap a discount on it, and hammer.
  - Callout: "That's not retargeting. That's price-cutting your way to bankruptcy." The prospect already said no and you're saying it louder. "Don't pull your pants down on price."
- **The right way: ask why they didn't buy.** There are typically three reasons someone clicks, lands and doesn't convert:
  1. They had an objection you didn't address.
  2. They didn't believe the claims.
  3. The offer wasn't right for them (wrong product, price point or timing).

## Page 67 (footer 66): Section 28: When an Ad Wins: The Promotion Process
- You know an ad is a winner when Meta starts allocating it a meaningful share of the Launch campaign's spend.
- When that happens:
  - **Leave the original active in Launch.** Don't pause it, because the platform's learning is bound to that ad ID.
  - **Duplicate the winner into every adset of Scale:** one copy per landing page/whitelist page combination.
  - **Feed the winner into the AI variant engine (Section 14).** Generate 50+ avatar-specific variants and launch them in the Launch campaign for the next round of testing.
  - **Clone the winner with niche/demographic keywords (Section 13).** One word swap per duplicate, launched alongside the original.
  - Update the destination URL and FB page in each Scale duplicate as required.
  - Apply the inflated budget rule (2× expected spend) on the Scale campaign.
  - Set the ROAS Goal at (Target ROAS ÷ 1.15) × 1.10 for the Scale duplicates.
  - Wait at least 7 days before sculpting and let the algorithm stabilise.

## Page 68 (footer 67): Section 29: When an Ad Dies: Diagnose Before You Delete
- Before killing an ad that's slowing down, ask three questions:
  1. Is this attribution lag? (Is actual ROAS being misrepresented by purchase-day attribution?)
  2. Is this an account-wide issue? (Are all ads slowing, meaning a CPM, seasonal or landing-page problem, not an ad problem?)
  3. Is this fatigue? (Has frequency climbed above 3.0 in the audience?)
- Actions:
  - If 1 → wait 3 more days.
  - If 2 → fix the upstream problem, not the ad.
  - If 3 → don't kill the ad. Run it back through the AI variant engine (Section 14) and ship 20–50 fresh clones of the winner before it dies completely. "The DNA still works; the surface just needs to change."
- "Killing winning ads too fast is one of the most expensive mistakes we see."

## Page 69 (footer 68): Section 30: Track the Metric That Actually Matters
- "This is the section that separates people who run ads from people who run businesses." Cost per lead, CPA and Ads Manager ROAS are amateur metrics: useful for diagnostics, useless for scaling.
- **The problem:** Meta's dashboard shows one number and your bank account shows another. Both can be true. Optimising for the dashboard number can "optimize yourself into bankruptcy".
- **What to actually track:**
  1. **Blended ROAS / MER at the business level.** Not Meta- or Google-reported ROAS, but total revenue ÷ total ad spend across every channel. Better still is aMER: the same calculation counting only new customers. That's the real lens on whether ads grow the business. Most accounts have an aMER materially lower than reported ROAS, and that gap is where decisions go wrong.
  2. **Net free cash flow on a 30-day window.** Money in vs money back after refunds, COGS, fulfilment and platform fees, measured over 30 days. It's the only number that gives confidence to scale and the only one that pays rent, salaries and dividends. "You can't pay anyone with a ROAS percentage."

## Page 70 (footer 69): Section 30 cont.: Maximum profitable CAC / The trap / Numbers Day
- **3. Maximum profitable CAC.** Sit down with your accountant, look at real margins, and calculate the most you can spend to acquire a customer and still hit your profit target. Optimise toward that, not an arbitrary CPL you got comfortable with at $500/month spend and now refuse to update.
- **The trap most people fall into:** "I tried spending more, but the ROAS went down." ROAS drops because you're acquiring more expensive customers at the margin, which is how scaling works. The right question is not "did the ROAS percentage drop?" but "did more net cash hit my bank account this month?"
  - Example: going from $10k/month at 15x ROAS to $100k/month at 5x ROAS is usually right. Revenue rises from $150k to $500k. "Pay your team with the $350k difference."
- **Numbers Day, the ritual:** block three hours a month, recurring, same day every month. Just you, your numbers, your accounting software and your P&L: no data analyst, no media buyer. Look at the actual numbers and know them firsthand.
  - Author has done this for ten years and says almost no business activity returns more. Confidence to scale comes from data, and speed and conviction come from confidence. It's not the sexiest thing on the calendar, but it moves the money needle like almost nothing else.
- **Action step:** put a recurring 3-hour block on the first Friday of every month titled "Numbers Day". Show up and don't skip it. The first one will be uncomfortable; by month three it'll be the highest-leverage three hours of your month.
